On April 30, 1897, in London, at an evening lecture, Thomson said that there is something smaller than the atom. This episode starts from that evening: why Hertz did not see cathode rays bent by an electric field, what kind of man Thomson was, why the Nobel Prize says "the conduction of electricity by gases", and how, with only a cup of charge, a little heat and one curve, he worked out the ratio of this thing's mass to its charge; then we work these steps out for ourselves, then tell of the 1906 prize, and finally how the "corpuscle" became the "electron".

## Somewhat startling

Near the end he said: the assumption of a state of matter more finely subdivided than the atom of an element is a somewhat startling one.

He gave these things smaller than atoms a name: "corpuscles". Atoms, he said, might be made up of corpuscles and holes, the holes being predominant.

Three weeks later an electrical trade weekly printed the whole lecture. In the same issue the physicist George FitzGerald replied that the rays might be free electrons, without our having to think that atoms were being split, nor consequently that we are on the track of the alchemists.

Yet at the end of his piece he wrote: I may express a hope that Professor Thomson is quite right in his by no means impossible hypothesis. If so, it would be the beginning of great advances in science, and might easily eclipse most of the other great discoveries of the nineteenth century.

Nine years later, in 1906, Thomson received the Nobel Prize in Physics. Yet the citation does not contain the word "electron"; it speaks of his theoretical and experimental investigations on the conduction of electricity by gases.

What were the "corpuscles" he spoke of that evening? How did he know they were smaller than atoms? And why did the Nobel Prize step around them?

## Two volts

As we saw in the last episode, cathode rays are something that shoots out of the cathode of a discharge tube. As to what they were, the German side held them to be waves in the ether, the English side a stream of negatively charged particles.

If they were charged particles, two charged metal plates on either side should bend them. In 1883 Hertz tried exactly this. He wrote in his paper that under the conditions of the experiment, the cathode ray was not deflected by any electromotive force.

Then he asked: what are these particles? Are they atoms, or molecules, or matter in a still finer state of subdivision?

His answer, as the last episode also told, was that their ratio of mass to charge is only about a thousandth of that of the hydrogen ion in electrolysis, whatever gas is in the tube.

## The man

Joseph John Thomson was born in Manchester, England, on the 18th of December 1856.

His father ran the family business as a bookseller and publisher, specializing in rare and antique books.

In 1871 his father sent him to Owens College in Manchester, when he was only fourteen. Two years later his father died.

His father had meant to apprentice him to a locomotive works. He said so himself in 1932, at a banquet of the Institution of Mechanical Engineers in Cambridge.

But Professor Barker at the college advised him to give up the plan and try for a scholarship at Cambridge.

In 1876 he went up to Trinity College, Cambridge, to read mathematics. In the mathematics honors examination of 1880 he came second. First was Larmor. In the same year he became a Fellow of Trinity College.

At the end of 1884 Lord Rayleigh gave up the Cavendish chair of experimental physics at Cambridge, and his successor was Thomson, barely 28 years old.

Rayleigh is the man from the 1904 episode.

Even Rayleigh said later: my doubt was whether Thomson should be professor of experimental physics; he had done very little experimenting at that time. But he has shown since that it was right to appoint him. Glazebrook, who had hoped for the post, wrote to him: the news of your election was too great a surprise to me; I had looked on you as a mathematician, not an experimental physicist. Thomson himself said he had never regarded his candidature as a serious one.

Yet this professor of experimental physics was not good with his hands. His colleague Newall recalled that Thomson was very awkward with his fingers, and that it was very necessary not to encourage him to handle the instruments.

The apparatus was mostly set up by his assistant Everett, who also did the pumping. At the end of the 1897 paper Thomson thanked him, but spelled his name wrong.

In January 1890 he married Rose Paget. She had attended his lectures, and had also done research at the Cavendish.

### Two accounts side by side

On a few matters the sources do not agree, so we write down both sides.

As for the year he entered Owens College, the biography on the official Nobel website gives 1870; Rayleigh's biography gives 1871, when he was fourteen; and the curriculum vitae he filled in himself for the yearbook *Les Prix Nobel en 1906* says he studied "at Owens College Manchester from 1871 to 1876". He turned fourteen only in December 1870, so "entering at fourteen" fits 1871 better. The main text above uses 1871.

As for the year he became President of the Royal Society, the official biography gives 1916, and Trinity College gives 1915. Rayleigh's biography prints his letter of July 1915 accepting the nomination, and also writes that he stepped down on November 30, 1920, "at the end of the usual five years"; counting back five years gives 1915.

As for the year his Cavendish professorship ended, the official biography gives 1918. Yet in a letter of his own dated March 23, 1919, he speaks of this professorship as one "which I have resigned". He became Master of Trinity College in 1918, and only in 1919 handed the Cavendish over to Rutherford.

On the knighthood, the official website gives 1908. The issue of the *London Gazette* of December 18, 1908, is more precise: effective from November 9, and conferred by the King on December 14.

## Making air conduct

But why did the Nobel Prize speak, of all things, of "the conduction of electricity by gases"? For that we go back to 1896.

In November 1896 Thomson and Rutherford published a paper on the passage of electricity through gases exposed to Röntgen rays. It was less than a year since Röntgen had discovered X-rays.

Air is normally an insulator. Put a voltage across two metal plates with air between them, and almost no current flows. But send X-rays through it and the air conducts; stop the X-rays, and it still conducts for a short while.

The next year, in 1899, he wrote that the charge on the negative carrier is about six times ten to the minus ten, equal to the positive charge carried by the hydrogen atom in electrolysis.

In the same paper he concluded that the mass of the negative carrier is only of the order of a thousandth of that of the hydrogen atom.

More precisely, he wrote about one point four times ten to the minus three of the hydrogen ion's, roughly one seven hundredth. Today's value is one in eighteen hundred and thirty six: the ratio he measured that time was a little over forty percent of today's.

And so he wrote: the production of negative electrification thus involves the splitting up of an atom.

How, then, was something smaller than an atom measured? Let's work it out.

## Working it out from the start

In the 1897 paper Thomson wrote that he had used two independent methods. Last time we worked through the crossed electric and magnetic fields. Here is his first method, the one behind Table 1.

$$
Q = Ne, \qquad W = \tfrac{1}{2}\,N m v^{2}
$$

Now add a magnetic field, and the rays bend into an arc. The magnetic force supplies the centripetal force; for the same field, the larger the radius, the harder the particles are to turn: m v over e equals the field times the radius. Thomson wrote I for the field times the radius.

![Now add a magnetic field, and the rays bend into an arc. The magnetic force supplies the centripetal force; for the same field, the larger the radius, the harder the particles are to turn: m v over e equals the field times the radius.](/content/images/2026/11/nobel-1906/1906-11-s5_arc.gif)

*Figure 11. Now add a magnetic field, and the rays bend into an arc. The magnetic force supplies the centripetal force; for the same field, the larger the radius, the harder the particles are to turn: m v over e equals the field times the radius.*

Thomson wrote the magnetic field as H and the radius as ρ; today the magnetic field is usually written B. The magnetic force $evB$ supplies the centripetal force:

$$
\frac{m v^{2}}{\rho} = e v B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{m v}{e} = B\rho \equiv I
$$

Put the three together, eliminate N and then v, and we get: m over e equals I squared times Q, divided by two W. The speed comes out too: two W divided by Q times I.

**Eliminating N.** Divide one equation by the other, and N is gone:

$$
\frac{W}{Q} = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m}{e}\,v^{2}
$$

**Eliminating v.** From the third equation, $v = I\,\dfrac{e}{m}$; put it in:

$$
\frac{W}{Q} = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{m}{e}\,I^{2}\Bigl(\frac{e}{m}\Bigr)^{2} = \frac{1}{2}\,I^{2}\,\frac{e}{m}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{m}{e} = \frac{I^{2} Q}{2W}, \qquad v = \frac{2W}{QI}
$$

On page 303 Thomson writes: Thus, if we know the values of Q, W, and I, we can deduce the values of v and m/e.

Take the first row of Table 1: for every coulomb of charge received, four thousand six hundred joules of energy came in; the field times the radius is two point three times ten to the minus four tesla meters. Putting these in, m over e is five point seven five times ten to the minus twelve kilograms per coulomb, or turned around, one point seven four times ten to the eleven coulombs per kilogram; the speed is forty million meters per second, about thirteen percent of the speed of light.

**In today's units.** Table 1 uses electromagnetic units in the centimeter-gram-second system: the first row reads W/Q = 4.6×10¹¹, I = 230. One electromagnetic unit of charge is 10 coulombs, one erg is 10⁻⁷ joules, one gauss is 10⁻⁴ tesla, and one centimeter is 10⁻² meters, so

$$
\frac{W}{Q} = 4.6\times10^{11}\ \frac{\mathrm{erg}}{\mathrm{emu}} = 4.6\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{J/C},
\qquad
I = 230\ \mathrm{G\cdot cm} = 2.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{T\cdot m}
$$

Putting these in:

$$
\frac{m}{e} = \frac{(2.3\times10^{-4})^{2}}{2\times 4.6\times10^{3}} = 5.75\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{kg/C},
\qquad
\frac{e}{m} = 1.74\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{C/kg}
$$

$$
v = \frac{2\times4.6\times10^{3}}{2.3\times10^{-4}} = 4.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 0.13\,c
$$

A joule per coulomb is a volt, so W/Q = 4600 means: the energy each particle brings in equals the energy it would gain by being accelerated through a voltage of 4600 volts. Working backward from today's charge-to-mass ratio of the electron, an electron accelerated through 4600 volts has a speed of

$$
v = \sqrt{2 \times 4600\ \mathrm{V} \times 1.7588\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{C/kg}} \approx 4.02\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m/s}
$$

which agrees with that row's own speed in the table. The charge-to-mass ratio of this row differs from today's value by only about one percent.

With three tubes and several gases, m over e always came out around ten to the minus seven, about a thousand times smaller than the hydrogen ion's ten to the minus four.

The m/e values across the whole table run from 0.32 to 1.0 (in units of 10⁻⁷), so the fact that the first row is so close to today's value cannot be taken to mean that he measured accurately.

**Compared with the hydrogen ion.** In electrolysis, releasing 1 mole of hydrogen atoms (about 1.008 grams) takes about 96485 coulombs of electricity, so

$$
\frac{m_{\mathrm{H}}}{e} = \frac{1.008\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg}}{96485\ \mathrm{C}} \approx 1.04\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{kg/C}
$$

larger than the first row's $5.75\times10^{-12}$ by a factor of around eighteen hundred.

Looking back today, the figures in Table 1 are closer to today's value, while Table 2 came out more than twice too large. Thomson himself gave one reason in the paper: outside the plates the field of the coils pointed the other way, he had not subtracted it, so the field that really acted was smaller than the one he used, and the m over e he calculated came out too large.

**The cloud of 1898.** To measure the charge on its own, he used another method. If each ion serves as the nucleus of one drop of water, then counting the drops counts the ions. The mass of water condensed in one cubic centimeter, divided by the mass of one drop, gives the number of drops n:

$$
n = \frac{\text{water condensed per cubic centimeter}}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^{3}\rho_{\text{water}}}
$$

The radius a of the drops comes from how fast the cloud falls. The formula he wrote on page 530 is

$$
v = \frac{2}{9}\,\frac{g a^{2}}{\mu}
$$

v is the speed at which the drops fall, μ is the viscosity of the gas, and g is the acceleration due to gravity. This is what is called Stokes's formula today, only he did not write that name; in the centimeter-gram-second system the density of water is 1, so it cannot be seen in the formula. Measure how much charge in all is carried by one cubic centimeter of the gas, divide by n, and that is the charge e of one ion.

The e he obtained was revised several times. In air the first result was 6.3×10⁻¹⁰, an uncorrected value. Without irradiation, expanding air also forms a little cloud; after subtracting those condensation nuclei, he got 7.4×10⁻¹⁰. The cloud formed without rays fell three times faster, so its drops were √3 times larger, and the same amount of water made 3^1.5 ≈ 5.2 times fewer drops; this is his "1 : 5·2". He then made five more runs in air, which after correction gave 7.6, 7.2, 8.4, 7.4 and 6.0; together with the 7.4 above, the mean of the six values is 7.3×10⁻¹⁰. Finally, a correction by the ratio of 9 to 8 for conduction at the walls of the vessel:

$$
e = \frac{8}{9} \times 7.3\times10^{-10} = 6.5\times10^{-10}\ \text{electrostatic units}
$$

One electrostatic unit is 3.336×10⁻¹⁰ coulombs, so

$$
6.5\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{esu} \approx 2.17\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}
$$

which is 1.35 times today's 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs. The "about 6×10⁻¹⁰" he gave in 1899 is 2.0×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs. The 1906 presentation speech used 3.4×10⁻¹⁰, which is 1.13×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, and this time it is smaller than today's value.

**The charge-to-mass ratio of 1899.** What he measured were the carriers of negative electricity knocked out by ultraviolet light, with a mean charge-to-mass ratio of 7.3×10⁶ electromagnetic units per gram, that is

$$
7.3\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{emu/g} = 7.3\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C/kg} \approx 0.415 \times 1.7588\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{C/kg}
$$

only a little over forty percent of today's value. And the mass is 1.4×10⁻³ of the hydrogen ion's; turned around, one seven hundred and fourteenth.

Without ever seeing a particle, a cup of charge, a little heat and one curve were enough to work out its ratio of mass to charge.

Nine years later, how was this judged in Stockholm?

## December 1906, Stockholm

As the last episode told, in 1905 Thomson had 13 nominations, more than anyone, yet the prize went to Lenard.

In 1906 the Physics prize drew 18 nominations, 8 of them for Thomson. Among those who nominated him were Rayleigh and Ramsay, the two men of the 1904 episode.

On the 12th of November 1906 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences decided to award that year's Physics prize to Joseph John Thomson.

The 10th of December, Stockholm. This time the laureate was there in person.

At the banquet that evening the Swedish physicist Knut Ångström toasted him in English, and he rose to reply.

The presentation speech said: even if Thomson has not actually beheld the atoms, he has nevertheless achieved work commensurable therewith, by having directly observed the quantity of electricity carried by each atom.

It also said that these small particles are called electrons, and that foremost among those who studied them were Lenard, last year's laureate, and Thomson.

It went on: all substances can give off negatively charged electrons, and the small positive particles are neutral atoms that have lost electrons. According to Thomson, the electricity that actually exists is negative electricity.

## What the archives say

Of those 18 nominations in 1906, Thomson had 8, and 7 of them nominated him alone: Adolf von Baeyer, Christian Christiansen, Oliver Lodge, John Lubbock, Ramsay, Rayleigh, Silvanus P. Thompson. The other 1 was from Emil Warburg, who nominated him together with Friedrich Kohlrausch.

Of the other 10, Boltzmann had 3, from Czerny, Planck, and the previous year's laureate Lenard; Boltzmann died in September of this year and never won the prize. Braun had 2; he won the prize in 1909. Lippmann and Poincaré had 3 between them, one of which was Becquerel's nomination of the two of them together with Righi. Von Hann had 1. And 1 was for Kelvin, and his nominator was Zeeman, the laureate of 1902.

Looking further back at Thomson himself: 1 in 1902, none in 1903, 1 in 1904, 13 in 1905 without winning, and 8 in 1906, when he won.

The archives record only who nominated whom. How the committee deliberated, the web pages of the nomination archive do not say.

## The prize money

The prize money was 138,536 Swedish kronor. The year before, Lenard's was 138,089 kronor.

So why did he not call it an "electron"? And where did that name come from?

## Afterwards

The name "electron" was coined in 1891 by George Johnstone Stoney of Ireland. He meant the units of charge carried by atoms, not particles that could travel on their own.

Thomson himself kept calling it a "corpuscle". In a book of his from 1904, "corpuscle" appears more than two hundred times and "electron" not once.

Rayleigh says in his biography that he kept using his own original word, "corpuscle"; only in his post-war writings did he stop speaking of corpuscles.

In the end it was others who settled the name. In 1909 Lorentz wrote in a book that it would be convenient to restrict the name of electrons to these negative particles, or, as Thomson calls them, corpuscles.

In 1904 he also drew a picture of the atom: a sphere of uniform positive electricity, with the negative corpuscles enclosed in it, arranged in rings.

In 1937 his son, George Thomson, also received the Nobel Prize in Physics, for the experimental discovery of the diffraction of electrons by crystals: electrons behaving like waves.

In his Nobel lecture the son mentioned his father only once: if among the many names associated with this advance I mention that of J. J. Thomson, I hope you will forgive a natural pride.

In 1895 Cambridge first let graduates of other universities come and do research here for a degree. Among the very first was the young man from New Zealand, Rutherford. He said later that while still at home he had read everything Thomson had written, and had made up his mind that this was the man he wanted to work under. Thomson wrote back that he could come and work in the Cavendish, and invited him to an interview.

Every morning Thomson went the round of the research workers, asking how they were getting on and suggesting what to try. If the work had got stuck, he would sit on a stool alongside, push his glasses up on his forehead, stay silent for a while, and then come out with a change to the apparatus or something new to try. The laboratory also had tea every afternoon. Rayleigh wrote that the tea hour was in many ways the best time in the laboratory day.

The 1906 presentation speech said that he had worked out the most important properties of these small charged particles aided by his numerous pupils. It also named in particular his pupil Wilson.

Among his students and the people who worked in his laboratory, several later won Nobel Prizes. Rutherford won the 1908 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, for his investigations into the disintegration of the elements and the chemistry of radioactive substances. Aston won the Chemistry prize too, in 1922: with a mass spectrograph he built himself, he found isotopes in a large number of non-radioactive elements. And the first to see neon split in two was Thomson.

He used "positive rays", that is, the positively charged stream in a discharge tube. Neon left two parabolas on his photographic plate, one corresponding to atomic weight 20 and one to 22, the latter much fainter. Rayleigh wrote that Thomson was the first to observe the resolution of neon in this way, and also suggested the true explanation, but was never very emphatic about it. Aston was his assistant from 1910 to 1913 and carried out much of the work on positive rays; the mass spectrograph Aston invented himself, after returning to the Cavendish in 1919.

In physics, there were Barkla, Bohr, Wilson, Richardson, Appleton and Born. In 1927 Wilson, the man in that photograph, won the Nobel Prize in Physics too, for making the paths of charged particles visible by the condensation of vapor. We will come to each of their stories later in this series.

He ran the Cavendish for more than thirty years, and the man who took it over from him was Rutherford. From 1915 to 1920 he was President of the Royal Society. In 1918 he became Master of Trinity, and remained Master until his death.

Thomson died in Cambridge on the 30th of August 1940. His ashes lie in the nave of Westminster Abbey, near Newton and Rutherford.

On that Friday evening in 1897, he called the idea of something smaller than an atom a somewhat startling one.

Today we call it the electron.

## His other side

None of these things is told in the film; all of them come from *The Life of Sir J. J. Thomson*, written in 1942 by the fourth Baron Rayleigh, with page numbers in parentheses. He was the son of the Lord Rayleigh of the 1904 episode, and was also Thomson's student.

His father kept a secondhand bookshop, and the son liked old books too. He often picked up old books on gardening from the bookstalls in the Cambridge market (p. 2). In 1899 the family moved to Holmleigh on West Road, where the garden was much larger; this was the main reason for the move. Rayleigh wrote: next to his family and the Cavendish Laboratory, the garden was very near J.J.'s heart (p. 130).

At university he did not row, nor play cricket or football, but all his life he loved watching them: cricket, particularly the matches at Fenner's, and Rugby football. In summer he played lawn tennis (p. 10).

He was fond of talking about psychical research. For many years his name was listed among the members of Council of the Society for Psychical Research, and later as a Vice-President (p. 254).

In July 1915 the First Lord of the Admiralty, Balfour, announced the setting up of a "Board of Invention and Research". The main committee had four members: Lord Fisher as Chairman, Thomson, Parsons and Beilby. Rayleigh wrote that the most successful work of the Board was the development of anti-submarine listening devices, carried out first at Hawkscraig and afterwards at Harwich under William Bragg (pp. 179, 181).

## A few common claims

**"Thomson won the Nobel Prize for discovering the electron."** The reason given for the prize is his theoretical and experimental investigations on the conduction of electricity by gases; there is no "electron" in it. The presentation speech, on the other hand, did speak of electrons. His 1897 paper does not contain the word "electron" either; it uses "corpuscles".

**"The plum pudding model was proposed by Thomson."** The model is his; the name is not. His own words in 1904 were: the atoms of the elements consist of a number of negatively electrified corpuscles enclosed in a sphere of uniform positive electrification. A search of the 1904 volume of the *Philosophical Magazine* and of Rayleigh's biography turns up no "pudding". Comparing something to a pudding goes back even earlier: Tait's *Properties of Matter* of 1885 says that matter is intensely heterogeneous, like a plum-pudding, for instance, or a mass of brick-work; this is said of matter in general, not of the atom. According to a 2013 article by Hon and Goldstein, the first to attach "plum pudding" to Thomson's atom was an unsigned article in *Merck's Report* of December 1906; we did not find the original of that article, so this point is secondhand.

**"Seven of his students won the Nobel Prize."** There is no fixed list. In the biographies on the official Nobel website, those explicitly said to have worked under him are Rutherford, Barkla, Aston, Bohr, Appleton and Born (Born stayed only a short time); the presentation speech calls Wilson his pupil; Richardson did research with him at the Cavendish. Different sources give counts anywhere from six to eleven. The Trinity College web page says seven people won the physics prize, but Rutherford and Aston won the chemistry prize. The official website has never given a number. So the main text says only "several".

**"The father proved the electron is a particle, the son proved the electron is a wave."** Neither prize citation says this. 1906 is the conduction of electricity by gases; 1937 is the diffraction of electrons by crystals. This contrast is a later summary; it is fine to tell as a story, but it is not the reason for either prize.
